Module 2.1 • ~45 min

🏗️ Agentic systems architecture

Organize runtime, components, states and boundaries between deterministic and agentic parts.

What is it:

system map with runtime, orchestration, agent, tools and storage.

Why learn:

Without clear architecture, bugs become mystery and maintenance becomes improvisation.

Key concepts:

runtime, orchestrator, storage and execution boundary.

What is it:

operational sequence that transforms trigger into delivered result.

Why learn:

allows you to define where to validate, measure and stop the flow.

Key concepts:

input, stage, output and checkpoint.

What is it:

places where the system chooses the next action, source or strategy.

Why learn:

distributing autonomy is right and more important than having more autonomy.

Key concepts:

branching, confidence, policy and escalation.

What is it:

layers that support reprocessing, resumption and scalability of the flow.

Why learn:

Without state and queue the system collapses in asynchronous scenarios or intermediate failures.

Key concepts:

queue, state store, retry queue and resume.

What is it:

criterion for deciding what remains in fixed code and what becomes the agent's decision.

Why learn:

this boundary defines cost, risk, predictability and depurability.

Key concepts:

determinism, flexibility, risk budget and policy.

What is it:

design a case with runtime, phases, state and decision points.

Why learn:

It is the necessary step to move away from isolated scripts and into operating systems.

Key concepts:

diagram, boundary, stage and ownership.

Module 2.2 • ~40 min

🧭 Reliable workflow design

Checkpoints, fallback, retry, human controls and failure recovery.

What is it:

break the flow into small parts with local acceptance criteria.

Why learn:

makes debug and regression much more manageable.

Key concepts:

phasing, gate and local validation.

What is it:

intermediate landmarks that record the useful state of the flow.

Why learn:

checkpoints avoid complete restarts and improve operational reliability.

Key concepts:

persist, checkpoint, resume and validation point.

What is it:

strategies for a new attempt or alternative route in the event of an error.

Why learn:

Much of the actual operation depends more on recovery than on a “happy path”.

Key concepts:

retry policy, backoff, fallback path and terminal failure.

What is it:

human gate to approve, correct or terminate execution at critical moments.

Why learn:

reduces risk, improves safety and increases operating confidence.

Key concepts:

approval, override, manual review and escalation.

What is it:

set of actions to diagnose, isolate and correct an error without breaking the rest of the system.

Why learn:

The system is only reliable if it can fail well and resume clearly.

Key concepts:

rollback, retry window, isolate and recover.

What is it:

design a flow with explicit failure and resumption routes.

Why learn:

makes the student responsible for reliability, not just “it works once”.

Key concepts:

robustness, checkpoint and fallback tree.

Module 2.3 • ~45 min

🔗 Real integrations

Connect files, APIs, banking, email and search into a coherent pipeline.

What is it:

manipulation of local or remote files as part of execution.

Why learn:

Many workflows live on artifacts and need standardized persistence.

Key concepts:

blob, path, artifact and versioning.

What is it:

use of external APIs considering limits, auth and response quality.

Why learn:

Poorly handled rate limit becomes a recurring failure and unnecessary cost.

Key concepts:

quota, backoff, batching and circuit breaker.

What is it:

record state, history, partial output or final result in structured storage.

Why learn:

Without organized persistence, the system loses traceability and reprocessing.

Key concepts:

schema, row, state table and audit trail.

What is it:

sending communication or alert as a final or intermediate step in the flow.

Why learn:

Notification is often the point where the workflow leaves the system and produces real impact.

Key concepts:

recipient, template, delivery and approval.

What is it:

collecting data from external structured or semi-structured sources.

Why learn:

weak research contaminates the rest of the flow and reduces the quality of the output.

Key concepts:

source quality, scrape, normalize and citation.

What is it:

build a simple pipeline combining input, persistence and output.

Why learn:

trains real chaining between dependencies, error and result.

Key concepts:

pipeline, dependency, I/O and state handoff.

Module 2.4 • ~45 min

🧩 MCP and interoperability

Learn MCP architecture, resources, prompts, tools and server inspection.

What is it:

protocol to expose resources, prompts and tools in an interoperable way.

Why learn:

MCP organizes integrations in a more composable and standardized way.

Key concepts:

client, server, capability and protocol.

What is it:

types of elements that an MCP server can publish for client consumption.

Why learn:

allows you to decide what should become a resource, template or executable action.

Key concepts:

resource, prompt template, tool exposure and discoverability.

What is it:

exchange of initial information to declare available resources and limits.

Why learn:

reduces weak integration and makes behavior more predictable.

Key concepts:

handshake, advertised capabilities and compatibility.

What is it:

lean implementation to publish at least one useful resource or tool.

Why learn:

makes the student understand MCP as a concrete artifact, not just a concept.

Key concepts:

server entrypoint, registration and transport.

What is it:

use of inspection tools to check MCP server capabilities, errors and payloads.

Why learn:

allows you to quickly diagnose why an MCP integration is not responding as expected.

Key concepts:

inspection, payload, capability list and error surface.

What is it:

raise a minimum capacity via MCP and inspect its behavior.

Why learn:

gives the student a concrete basis for integrating MCP into larger projects later.

Key concepts:

registration, invocation and inspection.

Module 2.5 • ~45 min

🧪 Evals, traces and debugging

Build measurable quality with dataset, graders, traces and regression control.

What is it:

collection of representative inputs to validate system behavior.

Why learn:

Without a test set, there is no comparison between versions.

Key concepts:

dataset, sample, edge case and baseline.

What is it:

objective or subjective evaluation mechanism of the agent's output.

Why learn:

without a grader, the team goes back for an opinion and “it looks good”.

Key concepts:

rubric, score, threshold and pass/fail.

What is it:

analysis of steps, tools and decisions recorded during execution.

Why learn:

Well-read trace shows whether the problem was context, tool, policy or model.

Key concepts:

step trace, tool call, decision point and failure surface.

What is it:

compare workflow, prompt or tool performance between iterations.

Why learn:

Without regression control, each local improvement can make 10 other scenarios worse.

Key concepts:

baseline, regression set, diff and score drift.

What is it:

iterate on instructions, tools and thresholds based on evaluation evidence.

Why learn:

mature and incremental optimization, don't guess in the dark.

Key concepts:

tuning, prompt delta, tool delta and measured improvement.

What is it:

assemble a mini dataset, run evaluation and diagnose a failure case via trace.

Why learn:

This skill differentiates demo from system engineering.

Key concepts:

grader, trace, eval set and regression check.

Module 2.6 • ~40 min

🛡️ Security and guardrails

Control scope, approvals, malicious input and destructive actions.

What is it:

clearly define what each tool can or cannot do.

Why learn:

operational risk increases more due to the scope of the tool than the model itself.

Key concepts:

least privilege, capability limit and role scope.

What is it:

operations that change the state irreversibly or at a relevant cost.

Why learn:

These actions need explicit barriers, not implicit trust.

Key concepts:

delete, mutate, spend, publish and approval gate.

What is it:

attempt to manipulate the agent through data or instructions coming from unreliable sources.

Why learn:

An agent that treats all text as trustworthy opens the way for dangerous behavior.

Key concepts:

trusted input, untrusted content, sanitization and policy override.

What is it:

treating external text as data to be analyzed, not as an order to be obeyed.

Why learn:

This simple separation reduces a lot of attack surface.

Key concepts:

content isolation, parser, trusted boundary and sanitize.

What is it:

review and approval controls that interrupt or redirect execution when necessary.

Why learn:

strong guardrail combines automatic policy with human judgment at the right points.

Key concepts:

review gate, override, manual approve and escalation path.

What is it:

evaluate a flow and define where limit, confirmation and isolation come into play.

Why learn:

Security needs to be designed, not added at the end.

Key concepts:

risk register, approval policy, unsafe action and trusted boundary.

Module 2.7 • ~40 min

🚦 Deploy and operation

Take the system to run on a scheduled or event basis with logs and metrics.

What is it:

run the workflow in a development environment and then in an operational environment.

Why learn:

Environment differences often create failures that do not appear in the local build.

Key concepts:

env parity, config, deploy target and secret management.

What is it:

triggers by time or event that start automatic execution.

Why learn:

Without an operational trigger, the flow remains just a manual demo.

Key concepts:

cron, webhook, queue trigger and event-driven.

What is it:

recording and visualization of system executions, failures and bottlenecks.

Why learn:

Without monitoring there is no serious maintenance or continuous improvement.

Key concepts:

log, metric, alert and trace link.

What is it:

operational measures that show whether the workflow is viable and sustainable.

Why learn:

Good engineering does not only look at output quality, but at cost and reliability of operation.

Key concepts:

latency, failure rate, unit cost and SLA.

What is it:

Simple scripts for diagnosis, restart, rollback and escalation in case of an incident.

Why learn:

Mature operations depend on standardized incident response.

Key concepts:

incident response, rollback, escalation and recovery procedure.

What is it:

put a flow into automatic execution with minimal visibility of operation.

Why learn:

makes the student leave the local build and enter the real operating environment.

Key concepts:

deploy, scheduler, logs and health visibility.

Module 2.8 • ~55 min

🚀 Main engineering project

Track closure with an end-to-end agentic workflow, with assessment, security and operation.

What is it:

choosing a case that justifies integration, validation and observable operation.

Why learn:

the final project needs to synthesize the track and not just be a pretty demo.

Key concepts:

scope, value, risk and measurable output.

What is it:

assemble runtime, workflow, tools and persistence according to the needs of the case.

Why learn:

the student needs to transform drawing into a concrete and articulated system.

Key concepts:

implementation plan, flow, tool chain and storage.

What is it:

instrument execution to understand behavior, failure and cost.

Why learn:

final project without observability has not yet become a real system.

Key concepts:

trace link, logs, metrics and state record.

What is it:

apply risk limits, approvals and policies to the entire project.

Why learn:

the final project needs to show operational maturity, not just functional engineering.

Key concepts:

policy, approval, boundary and safe execution.

What is it:

run basic evals and read traces to defend the quality of the result.

Why learn:

without measure, the final project becomes a subjective demonstration.

Key concepts:

score, eval set, trace review and regression check.

What is it:

Technical demo with architecture, flow, risks, metrics and next steps.

Why learn:

A good engineer needs to explain the system he built and defend his tradeoffs.

Key concepts:

demo, architecture review, metrics and next iteration.
